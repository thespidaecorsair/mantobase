Myrmecomorphy

The act of mimicing an ant is called myrmecomorphy, and many insects have adapted to perform this to avoid predators. For mantises, many nymphs start off mimicing ants to avoid attention while they develop. Others stay like this entirely!
Some species, as an example, are:

Odontomantis Planiceps

This first one is an Asian Ant Mantis (Odontomantis planiceps). it is a small mantis, ranging no longer than 2cm (for females, as males grow max 1.4cm.)

Mimicry

Many bugs, not just mantises, use mimicry to blend in with their environment, interestingly, not all of them use it for the same outcome . . .

Hymenopus coronatus - Orchid-blossom Mantis / Walking Flower Mantis

This species is a SouthEast Asian mantis from the tropical forests of places like Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Thailand, Vietnam and more. It mimics the partso f an orchid flower, with its walking legs resembling the petals. This appearance helps them stay still enough in specific positions to resemble an orchid flower, so that unsuspecting insects can approach and become their meal.

Interestingly, they also have the ability to change from pink and brown depending on the humidity. Other species of mantis do this too!

This type of mimicry is considered aggressive mimicry, as its otherwise harmless intentions of mimicry (like other species, eg a stick bug/phasmid resembling a stick to not be eaten) are used for hunting/predatory purposes.


Phyllocrania Paradoxa - Ghost Mantis

This is a small species from Africa which mimics a dead, burnt up leaf. This mimicry helps them adapt to their environment and avoid predators primarily, unlike the Orchid-blossom mantis, which uses it to blend in for predatory reasons.

Alongside this mimicry, the bigger nymphs and adult females play dead (thanatosis), by acting like that same dead leaf. (Interestingly, the smaller nymphs and males tend to run/fly away!)


Raptorial Forelegs

'Raptorial' is used to describe the idea of a predatory modification, but usually it is also used to describe a part of the body which is used for grasping prey during consumption. The surface for gripping onto the pray is between the two leg segments that fold into themselves.



This occurs in mantids, but also can occur in mantis shrimp, Matispidae (mantidflys), Belostomatidae (Giant water bugs), Nepidae (water scorpions), Naurocidae (creeping water bugs), and some flies and thrips.

Compound Eyes

Compound eyes are something most if not all invertebrate have, apart from their simple eyes as well. Insects especially have 2 or 3 simple eyes, and usually have 2 sets of compound eyes. These eyes are made up of thousands of independent 'eyes'.

Mantises have a significantly exceptional vision to make sure they can keep an eye out on predators and prey alike. Mantises are also seen to have pseudopupils, a dark spot that moves across the eye as the mantis moves. It seems like it is its pupil, but that is because of the way the light passes through.

All compound eye 'eyes', called ommatidia, are on different angles, and not all absorb/reflect light respectively, and it can appear like a real pupil telling us where the mantis is looking. In reality, it is looking everywhere else just as much! It just depends on where their head is facing.
